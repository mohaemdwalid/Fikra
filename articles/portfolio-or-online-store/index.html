Business websites

Does your business need a portfolio or an online store?

By FIKRA · Updated 16 October 2026 · 2 min read

A portfolio helps someone judge your work; a store helps them buy a defined item. Decide whether the purchase needs a conversation first.

In this guide
  1. Use the transaction to decide
  2. Understand the decision
  3. Prepare the right evidence
  4. Plan the next step
  5. Match the contact route to the work

Use the transaction to decide

A project whose scope changes after a discussion may need strong examples and a quote form. A standard product with known options can suit checkout. You can combine both, but explain which items are examples and which are for sale. A portfolio entry needs context about your contribution; a product needs price, availability and fulfilment information. Do not add a cart merely because it makes the site feel more advanced.

Understand the decision

If projects vary by scope, show examples with context and a useful enquiry route. If products have clear options and fulfilment rules, a store may reduce manual ordering. Some businesses need both, with distinct navigation for each task.

Prepare the right evidence

Portfolio entries should explain the brief, your actual contribution and approved outcomes. Product pages need prices, options, availability and delivery information. Avoid presenting mockups as completed client work or case-study percentages without supporting records.

Plan the next step

Decide who responds to a project enquiry and who fulfils a product order. Evaluate technology only after those workflows are defined. Adding a cart to an unclear service offer can introduce confusion rather than useful automation.

Match the contact route to the work

For a tailored service, a project page can explain the brief, scope and next step without adding a cart. For a fixed deliverable with a clear price, direct purchase may be useful. Decide how customers buy from you today before choosing ecommerce features that introduce unnecessary administration.
